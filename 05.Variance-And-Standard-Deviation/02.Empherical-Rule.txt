============================================================
  THE EMPIRICAL RULE — CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
============================================================

So far we can compute a mean and a standard deviation.
But when we report a simulation result, a single number
isn't enough. We need to say how confident we are in it.

That's what confidence intervals are for.

------------------------------------------------------------
CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
------------------------------------------------------------

Instead of estimating an unknown parameter by a single value
(e.g. the mean of a set of trials), a confidence interval
provides a range that is likely to contain the true value,
along with a confidence level that it actually does.

  Example:
    "The return of betting a pocket 10,000 times in European
     Roulette is -3.3%, with a margin of error of ±3.5%
     at a 95% confidence level."

  What this means:
    If you ran an infinite number of trials of 10,000 bets each:
      - Your expected average return would be -3.3%
      - Your return would fall between -6.8% and +0.2%
        approximately 95% of the time

  What it does NOT mean:
    It does NOT mean there is a 95% probability the true value
    is in that specific interval. The true value is fixed —
    either it's in the interval or it isn't. The 95% refers
    to the procedure: if you repeated this many times, 95% of
    the intervals you compute would contain the true value.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE EMPIRICAL RULE (68-95-99.7 Rule)
------------------------------------------------------------

Under the assumption that data follows a normal distribution
(discussed more in the next file):

  68%  of data falls within  1    standard deviation of the mean
  95%  of data falls within  1.96 standard deviations of the mean
  99.7% of data falls within 3    standard deviations of the mean

  Visually:

  |←—— 68% ——→|
  |←———— 95% ————→|
  |←———————99.7%———————→|
  
  ... —3σ —— —2σ —— —1σ —— μ —— +1σ —— +2σ —— +3σ ...

The 95% level (1.96 σ) is the one used most in practice.
When someone says "margin of error" in statistics or news,
they almost always mean a 95% confidence interval.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE FORMULA
------------------------------------------------------------

For a set of N simulation trials with mean μ and
standard deviation σ, the 95% confidence interval is:

  CI = μ ± 1.96 × (σ / sqrt(N))

The term (σ / sqrt(N)) is called the STANDARD ERROR.
It is the standard deviation of the sample mean itself —
it measures how much the mean would vary across repeated
experiments.

  Standard Error = σ / sqrt(N)

  As N increases → standard error shrinks → interval narrows
  → you become more confident in your estimate

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY sqrt(N) AND NOT N?
------------------------------------------------------------

If you average N independent measurements, the variance
of the average is σ²/N (variances add for independent variables,
and averaging divides variance by N).

  Var(mean) = σ² / N
  SD(mean)  = σ / sqrt(N)   ← standard error

This is why doubling precision requires 4× the samples.
To cut the interval in half, you need 4× the data.

------------------------------------------------------------
APPLYING THIS TO ROULETTE
------------------------------------------------------------

This is exactly what will be implemented in the next file.

For the roulette simulation, the plan is:
  1. Run the roulette game for numSpins, repeat for numTrials
  2. Collect the return from each trial
  3. Compute the mean return across all trials
  4. Compute the standard deviation of those returns
  5. Compute the 95% confidence interval:
       mean ± 1.96 × (std / sqrt(numTrials))
  6. Print: "Mean return = X%, 95% CI: [lower, upper]"

Expected behavior:
  - At low numSpins (100)     → wide confidence interval
  - At high numSpins (1,000,000) → very tight confidence interval
  - European/American wheels will have their CI centered
    around the true house edge (-2.7% and -5.26%)

------------------------------------------------------------
THE ASSUMPTION — NORMAL DISTRIBUTION
------------------------------------------------------------

The empirical rule only applies when the data is
approximately normally distributed (bell-shaped).

For roulette simulations this holds because of the
CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM:
  Even if individual spin outcomes are not normally distributed,
  the MEAN of a large number of those outcomes will be.

As long as numSpins is large enough (generally >30 is fine,
>1000 is very reliable), you can safely apply the empirical
rule to the distribution of trial means.

------------------------------------------------------------
QUICK REFERENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

  Standard Deviation (σ)  — spread of raw data
  Standard Error (SE)     — spread of the sample mean = σ/sqrt(N)
  Confidence Interval     — mean ± 1.96 × SE  (for 95%)
  Margin of Error         — the ± part = 1.96 × SE
