============================================================
  THE EMPIRICAL RULE AND CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
============================================================

Computing a mean and standard deviation describes the data
that has been observed. The more important question in
simulation is how well that observed data characterizes
an unknown true value. Confidence intervals are the
formal answer to that question.

------------------------------------------------------------
CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
------------------------------------------------------------

Rather than estimating an unknown parameter with a single value,
a confidence interval provides a range that is likely to contain
the true value, paired with a confidence level specifying how
often such intervals actually do contain it.

  Example:
    "The return of betting a single pocket 10,000 times in
     European Roulette is −3.3%, with a margin of error of
     ±3.5% at a 95% confidence level."

  Interpretation:
    If this experiment were repeated an infinite number of times,
    each time computing this interval from 10,000 spins:
      - The average return across all experiments would be −3.3%
      - Approximately 95% of the computed intervals would contain
        the true expected return

  A common misreading:
    A 95% confidence interval does NOT mean there is a 95%
    probability that the true value lies within this specific
    interval. The true value is fixed — it either falls inside
    or it does not. The 95% refers to the reliability of the
    procedure over repeated application, not to the probability
    of any single interval.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE EMPIRICAL RULE (68 — 95 — 99.7)
------------------------------------------------------------

Under the assumption that the data follows a normal distribution:

  68%   of observations fall within 1    standard deviation of the mean
  95%   of observations fall within 1.96 standard deviations of the mean
  99.7% of observations fall within 3    standard deviations of the mean

  Illustrated:

  |←—— 68% ——→|
  |←———— 95% ————→|
  |←——————— 99.7% ———————→|

  ... −3σ —— −2σ —— −1σ —— μ —— +1σ —— +2σ —— +3σ ...

The 95% band (1.96σ) is the standard in applied statistics.
When a poll or study reports a "margin of error," it is almost
universally referring to a 95% confidence interval.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE CONFIDENCE INTERVAL FORMULA
------------------------------------------------------------

For N simulation trials producing a mean μ and standard
deviation σ, the 95% confidence interval is:

  CI = μ ± 1.96 × (σ / sqrt(N))

The quantity σ / sqrt(N) is the STANDARD ERROR — the standard
deviation of the sample mean across hypothetical repeated
experiments. It measures not how spread out the raw data is,
but how much the computed mean itself would vary if the
experiment were run again.

  Standard Error = σ / sqrt(N)

As N increases:
  → Standard error decreases
  → Confidence interval narrows
  → Estimate becomes more precise

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY sqrt(N) AND NOT N
------------------------------------------------------------

When N independent measurements are averaged, variance
divides by N due to the additive property of variance:

  Var(mean of N values) = σ² / N

Taking the square root to obtain standard deviation:

  SD(mean) = σ / sqrt(N)   ← this is the standard error

This derivation explains why precision scales with the square
root of sample size rather than linearly. To halve the
confidence interval, the sample size must be quadrupled.
To reduce it by a factor of 10, the sample size must increase
by a factor of 100.

------------------------------------------------------------
APPLICATION TO THE ROULETTE SIMULATION
------------------------------------------------------------

The roulette simulation in the next file applies this
framework directly. The procedure is:

  1. Run the game for numSpins, record the mean return per spin
  2. Repeat for numTrials independent trials
  3. Compute the mean of the trial returns
  4. Compute the standard deviation of the trial returns
  5. Compute the 95% confidence interval:
       mean ± 1.96 × (std / sqrt(numTrials))
  6. Report: mean return and the interval bounds

The expected behavior across spin counts:
  - At numSpins = 1,000     → wide interval, noisy estimate
  - At numSpins = 1,000,000 → narrow interval, precise estimate
  - European/American wheels should have their intervals
    centered near the true house edge (−2.70% and −5.26%)

This structure directly tests whether the empirical rule
holds in practice for this class of simulation.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE ASSUMPTION — NORMAL DISTRIBUTION
------------------------------------------------------------

The empirical rule is valid only when the data is
approximately normally distributed.

For roulette simulations, this condition is satisfied
through the CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM:

  Even when individual spin outcomes are not normally
  distributed (each spin is a binary win/loss), the mean
  of a sufficiently large number of such outcomes will
  follow a normal distribution.

In practice, numSpins greater than 30 is generally sufficient
for the approximation to hold. At numSpins = 1,000 or more,
the normal approximation is very reliable.

------------------------------------------------------------
QUICK REFERENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

  Standard Deviation (σ) — spread of individual observations
  Standard Error (SE)    — spread of the sample mean = σ / sqrt(N)
  Confidence Interval    — mean ± 1.96 × SE  (95% level)
  Margin of Error        — the ± term = 1.96 × SE
