============================================================
  QUANTIFYING VARIATION IN DATA
  Variance and Standard Deviation
============================================================

When we run a Monte Carlo simulation, we don't just want
the average result — we want to know how much the results
vary. Two simulations can have the same mean but wildly
different spreads. Variance and standard deviation measure
that spread.

------------------------------------------------------------
MEAN (QUICK RECAP)
------------------------------------------------------------

  Mean (μ) = sum of all values / number of values

  Example: [2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9]
  Mean = (2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9) / 8 = 40 / 8 = 5

  The mean tells you the center. It says nothing about spread.

------------------------------------------------------------
VARIANCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Variance measures the average squared distance of each
data point from the mean.

  Variance (σ²) = (1/N) × Σ (xᵢ - μ)²

  For the example above (mean = 5):
    Deviations:    -3, -1, -1, -1, 0, 0, 2, 4
    Squared:        9,  1,  1,  1, 0, 0, 4, 16
    Sum of squares: 32
    Variance = 32 / 8 = 4

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY SQUARE THE DEVIATIONS?
------------------------------------------------------------

Your instinct is right to question this — why not just use
the absolute value of (xᵢ - μ)?

  Option 1 — Absolute deviation:  |xᵢ - μ|
  Option 2 — Squared deviation:   (xᵢ - μ)²

Both give positive numbers. Both measure distance from the mean.
So why do we square?

Three reasons:

  1. MATHEMATICAL CONVENIENCE
     Squared functions are smooth and differentiable everywhere.
     Absolute value has a sharp corner at zero — calculus breaks
     there. Squaring makes the math tractable for proofs,
     optimization, and the normal distribution derivation.

  2. PENALIZING OUTLIERS MORE HEAVILY
     Squaring amplifies large deviations more than small ones.
     A point 10 units away contributes 100 to variance.
     A point 1 unit away contributes only 1.
     This is actually a feature — it makes variance sensitive
     to extreme values, which is often what you care about.

  3. VARIANCE ADDS CLEANLY
     Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)  (for independent X, Y)
     This additive property does not hold for mean absolute deviation.
     It's what makes variance useful in probability theory.

  The tradeoff: variance is in squared units, which is why we
  take the square root to get standard deviation.

------------------------------------------------------------
STANDARD DEVIATION
------------------------------------------------------------

  Standard Deviation (σ) = sqrt(Variance) = sqrt(σ²)

  From the example: σ = sqrt(4) = 2

Standard deviation is just variance brought back to the
original units of the data. If your data is in dollars,
variance is in dollars-squared (meaningless), but standard
deviation is in dollars (interpretable).

------------------------------------------------------------
THE OUTLIER EFFECT
------------------------------------------------------------

Because deviations are squared, outliers have a
disproportionately large effect on variance.

  Dataset A: [4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 6]   → mean = 5, σ ≈ 0.5
  Dataset B: [4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 50]  → mean ≈ 10.5, σ ≈ 15.1

One extreme value completely dominates the variance in B.
This is important in simulations — a few extreme outcomes
can inflate variance and make your confidence intervals
much wider than they would otherwise be.

------------------------------------------------------------
STANDARD DEVIATION RELATIVE TO THE MEAN
------------------------------------------------------------

Standard deviation alone means nothing without context.
A σ of 10 is huge if the mean is 5, but tiny if the mean is 10,000.

This is why we use the COEFFICIENT OF VARIATION (CV):

  CV = σ / μ   (expressed as a percentage)

  Example:
    Mean = 100, σ = 10  → CV = 10%  (low variation)
    Mean = 5,   σ = 10  → CV = 200% (very high variation)

In Monte Carlo, always look at standard deviation relative
to your mean estimate. A simulation result of π ≈ 3.14 with
σ = 0.001 is very tight. The same result with σ = 0.3 is not.

------------------------------------------------------------
SAMPLE VARIANCE vs POPULATION VARIANCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Population variance (when you have ALL data):
  σ² = (1/N) × Σ (xᵢ - μ)²

Sample variance (when you have a SUBSET of data):
  s² = (1/(N-1)) × Σ (xᵢ - x̄)²

The (N-1) denominator is called Bessel's correction.
It corrects for the fact that a sample mean is slightly
closer to the sample data than the true population mean is —
which would otherwise cause variance to be underestimated.

In Monte Carlo simulations, you're almost always working
with samples, so use (N-1) in practice.

------------------------------------------------------------
PUTTING IT TOGETHER
------------------------------------------------------------

  Mean             → WHERE the data is centered
  Variance         → HOW MUCH the data is spread (squared units)
  Standard Deviation → HOW MUCH the data is spread (original units)
  CV               → HOW MUCH spread relative to the mean

  All four together give you a complete picture of a dataset.
