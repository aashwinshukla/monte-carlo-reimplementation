============================================================
  QUANTIFYING VARIATION IN DATA
  Variance and Standard Deviation
============================================================

A simulation result reported as a single number — a mean — is
incomplete. Two simulations can produce identical means while
differing dramatically in how much their individual results
scatter around that mean. Variance and standard deviation are
the tools used to measure and communicate that scatter.

------------------------------------------------------------
MEAN
------------------------------------------------------------

  Mean (μ) = sum of all values / number of values

  Example: [2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9]
  μ = (2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9) / 8 = 40 / 8 = 5

The mean identifies the center of a distribution.
It carries no information about how spread out the data is.

------------------------------------------------------------
VARIANCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Variance measures the average squared distance of each
data point from the mean.

  Variance (σ²) = (1/N) × Σ (xᵢ − μ)²

  For the example above (μ = 5):
    Deviations:      −3, −1, −1, −1,  0,  0,  2,  4
    Squared:          9,  1,  1,  1,  0,  0,  4, 16
    Sum of squares:  32
    Variance = 32 / 8 = 4

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY DEVIATIONS ARE SQUARED
------------------------------------------------------------

The natural question is why deviations are squared rather than
simply taking the absolute value — both approaches yield
positive numbers representing distance from the mean.

The choice of squaring over absolute value is justified on
three grounds:

  1. MATHEMATICAL TRACTABILITY
     The squared function is smooth and differentiable at
     every point, including zero. The absolute value function
     has a non-differentiable corner at zero, which prevents
     the use of calculus-based optimization and complicates
     formal proofs. Squaring makes the mathematics tractable.

  2. OUTLIER SENSITIVITY
     Squaring disproportionately amplifies large deviations.
     A point 10 units from the mean contributes 100 to variance.
     A point 1 unit away contributes only 1.
     This is not a flaw — it is a deliberate property that
     makes variance sensitive to extreme values, which are
     often the most consequential observations in a dataset.

  3. ADDITIVE DECOMPOSITION
     For independent random variables X and Y:
       Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)
     This additive property does not hold for mean absolute
     deviation. It is what makes variance compositionally
     useful across probability theory and statistics.

The cost of squaring is that variance exists in squared units,
rendering it dimensionally uninterpretable in isolation. This
is resolved by taking the square root.

------------------------------------------------------------
STANDARD DEVIATION
------------------------------------------------------------

  Standard Deviation (σ) = sqrt(Variance)

  From the example: σ = sqrt(4) = 2

Standard deviation restores the measurement to the original
units of the data. If the data is denominated in dollars,
variance is in dollars-squared — a unit without direct meaning.
Standard deviation returns the measurement to dollars, making
it directly comparable to the mean.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE OUTLIER EFFECT
------------------------------------------------------------

Because deviations are squared, a single extreme value can
dominate variance entirely.

  Dataset A: [4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 6]   → μ = 5,   σ ≈ 0.5
  Dataset B: [4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 50]  → μ ≈ 10.5, σ ≈ 15.1

One outlier in Dataset B has increased the standard deviation
by a factor of roughly 30. In the context of Monte Carlo
simulation, a small number of extreme trial outcomes can
inflate variance substantially, producing confidence intervals
far wider than the central tendency of results would suggest.

------------------------------------------------------------
STANDARD DEVIATION RELATIVE TO THE MEAN
------------------------------------------------------------

Standard deviation is only interpretable in relation to
the scale of the mean. A standard deviation of 10 is large
when the mean is 5, but negligible when the mean is 10,000.

This relative measure is formalized as the
COEFFICIENT OF VARIATION (CV):

  CV = σ / μ   (typically expressed as a percentage)

  Example:
    μ = 100, σ = 10  → CV = 10%  (low relative variation)
    μ = 5,   σ = 10  → CV = 200% (very high relative variation)

In Monte Carlo, a simulation estimate of π ≈ 3.14 with σ = 0.001
represents a tight, reliable result. The same estimate with
σ = 0.3 indicates the simulation has not yet converged.

------------------------------------------------------------
SAMPLE VARIANCE vs POPULATION VARIANCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Population variance — used when the full dataset is available:
  σ² = (1/N) × Σ (xᵢ − μ)²

Sample variance — used when working with a subset of data:
  s² = (1/(N−1)) × Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²

The (N−1) denominator is Bessel's correction. It compensates
for the fact that the sample mean is systematically closer
to the sample data than the true population mean is — which
would otherwise cause variance to be consistently underestimated.

Monte Carlo simulations operate on samples, so sample variance
with (N−1) is the appropriate measure in practice.

------------------------------------------------------------
SUMMARY
------------------------------------------------------------

  Mean               → where the data is centered
  Variance           → how much the data is spread (squared units)
  Standard Deviation → how much the data is spread (original units)
  Coefficient of Variation → spread relative to the mean

  These four quantities together provide a complete
  characterization of a dataset's central tendency and dispersion.
