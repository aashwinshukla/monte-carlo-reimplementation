============================================================
  HISTORY AND STORY OF MONTE CARLO SIMULATION
============================================================

------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE THE NAME COMES FROM
------------------------------------------------------------

Monte Carlo is a district in Monaco, famous for its casinos.
The method was named after it because of one simple reason:
randomness. Roulette wheels, dice, card shuffles — a casino
is a machine that generates random outcomes at scale.

The name was coined around 1947 by Nicholas Metropolis,
a physicist working at Los Alamos National Laboratory.
It was a code name — the work was classified.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE ORIGIN STORY
------------------------------------------------------------

The year is 1945. World War II has just ended.
The Manhattan Project scientists at Los Alamos are now
working on the hydrogen bomb.

Stanislaw Ulam, a Polish-American mathematician, is lying
in bed recovering from an illness. To pass the time, he
plays solitaire. He starts wondering: what is the probability
of a solitaire game being winnable? The combinations are
too complex to calculate by hand.

His thought: why not just play it out randomly, many times,
and count how often it wins?

He brought this idea to John von Neumann, one of the greatest
mathematicians of the 20th century. Von Neumann immediately
saw the potential — not just for card games, but for the
neutron diffusion problems they were trying to solve at Los Alamos.

How far does a neutron travel before hitting another nucleus?
The math is intractable. But you can simulate a neutron's
random walk millions of times and just observe what happens.

The method worked. Monte Carlo simulation was born.

ENIAC — one of the first electronic computers — was used to
run some of the earliest Monte Carlo calculations in 1948.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE MONTE CARLO IS USED TODAY
------------------------------------------------------------

Finance:
  - Pricing options and derivatives
  - Simulating portfolio risk (Value at Risk)
  - Stress-testing banks against thousands of market scenarios

Physics:
  - Simulating particle collisions (used at CERN)
  - Modeling radiation transport in medical imaging
  - Quantum mechanics calculations

Engineering:
  - Reliability testing: "what is the probability this bridge fails?"
  - Simulating manufacturing defects
  - Aerospace trajectory planning

AI and Machine Learning:
  - Monte Carlo Tree Search — used in AlphaGo to evaluate moves
  - Reinforcement learning policy estimation
  - Bayesian inference

Medicine:
  - Radiation therapy planning (dose distribution)
  - Drug trial outcome prediction
  - Epidemiological modeling (disease spread)

Climate Science:
  - Simulating atmospheric uncertainty
  - Projecting temperature ranges over decades

------------------------------------------------------------
WHEN TO USE MONTE CARLO
------------------------------------------------------------

Monte Carlo is the right tool when:

  1. The problem has randomness or uncertainty built into it
  2. The space of outcomes is too large to enumerate
  3. The function is too complex to integrate analytically
  4. You need a probability distribution, not just a single answer
  5. You're in high dimensions (10+) where grid methods collapse

Monte Carlo is NOT ideal when:
  - The problem has a clean analytical solution (just use math)
  - You need very high precision quickly (it converges slowly)
  - Computational resources are extremely limited

------------------------------------------------------------
ONE LINE SUMMARY
------------------------------------------------------------

  Monte Carlo: when the math is too hard, let randomness
  do the work — and do it enough times that the answer emerges.
