============================================================
  HISTORY AND STORY OF MONTE CARLO SIMULATION
============================================================

------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE THE NAME COMES FROM
------------------------------------------------------------

Monte Carlo is a district in Monaco, famous for its casinos.
The method was named after it for one reason: randomness.
Roulette wheels, dice, card shuffles — a casino is essentially
a machine that generates random outcomes at scale.

The name was coined around 1947 by Nicholas Metropolis,
a physicist working at Los Alamos National Laboratory.
It was a code name — the work was classified at the time.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE ORIGIN STORY
------------------------------------------------------------

The year is 1945. World War II has just ended. The Manhattan
Project scientists at Los Alamos are now working on the
hydrogen bomb.

Stanislaw Ulam, a Polish-American mathematician, is recovering
from an illness. To pass the time, he plays solitaire and begins
wondering: what is the probability that a given solitaire layout
is winnable? The combinatorial space is far too large to calculate
by hand.

The insight was simple: instead of computing it analytically,
play it out randomly many times and count how often it wins.

Ulam brought this idea to John von Neumann, one of the foremost
mathematicians of the 20th century. Von Neumann immediately
recognized the broader potential — not just for card games, but
for the neutron diffusion problems the team was trying to solve.

How far does a neutron travel before striking another nucleus?
The mathematics is intractable. But simulating a neutron's random
walk millions of times and observing the outcome distribution —
that is tractable. The method worked.

ENIAC, one of the first electronic computers, was used to run
some of the earliest Monte Carlo calculations in 1948.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE MONTE CARLO IS USED TODAY
------------------------------------------------------------

Finance:
  - Pricing options and derivatives
  - Simulating portfolio risk (Value at Risk)
  - Stress-testing financial institutions against
    thousands of market scenarios

Physics:
  - Simulating particle collisions (used at CERN)
  - Modeling radiation transport in medical imaging
  - Quantum mechanical calculations

Engineering:
  - Structural reliability: estimating the probability of failure
  - Simulating manufacturing defects and tolerances
  - Aerospace trajectory planning under uncertainty

AI and Machine Learning:
  - Monte Carlo Tree Search — used in AlphaGo to evaluate moves
  - Reinforcement learning policy estimation
  - Bayesian inference and posterior sampling

Medicine:
  - Radiation therapy treatment planning
  - Drug trial outcome prediction
  - Epidemiological modeling of disease spread

Climate Science:
  - Simulating atmospheric uncertainty
  - Projecting temperature distributions over decades

------------------------------------------------------------
WHEN MONTE CARLO IS THE RIGHT TOOL
------------------------------------------------------------

Monte Carlo is appropriate when:

  1. The problem contains inherent randomness or uncertainty
  2. The outcome space is too large to enumerate directly
  3. The integrand is too complex for closed-form analysis
  4. A probability distribution is needed, not just a point estimate
  5. The problem is high-dimensional, where grid methods collapse

Monte Carlo is not well-suited when:
  - A clean analytical solution exists
  - Very high precision is needed quickly (convergence is slow)
  - Computational resources are severely constrained

------------------------------------------------------------
ONE LINE SUMMARY
------------------------------------------------------------

  Monte Carlo: when the math is too hard, let randomness
  do the work — and repeat it enough times that the
  answer emerges from the noise.
