============================================================
  INTRODUCTION TO MONTE CARLO SIMULATION
============================================================

To predict a stock price, every possible outcome must be considered.
To simulate how a particle moves after a collision, every interaction
matters. The space of outcomes is vast and continuous — it simply
cannot be enumerated.

This is where Monte Carlo Simulation offers an elegant idea:

  "Don't enumerate everything. Instead, randomly sample possibilities,
   compute the outcome for each sample, and average the results.
   The Law of Large Numbers guarantees convergence."

At 10 samples        → the result is noisy
At 1,000 samples     → the result is close
At 1,000,000 samples → the result is essentially exact

------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT MONTE CARLO ACTUALLY COMPUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Monte Carlo computes an EXPECTED VALUE — the average outcome
over a large number of random trials. Rather than solving a
problem analytically, it approximates the answer by observing
what happens across many random experiments and letting the
distribution speak for itself.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY TRADITIONAL INTEGRATION FAILS
------------------------------------------------------------

Traditional grid-based integration breaks down under three
conditions:

  1. The space is too large (too many dimensions)
  2. Probabilities are unknown or difficult to define analytically
  3. The function itself is too complex to integrate in closed form

The fundamental problem is known as the "Curse of Dimensionality":
grid-based methods require evaluation points that grow
EXPONENTIALLY with the number of dimensions.

  2 dimensions,   10 points each → 100 evaluations
  10 dimensions,  10 points each → 10,000,000,000 evaluations
  100 dimensions, 10 points each → more evaluations than atoms in the universe

Monte Carlo breaks this curse entirely. The number of samples
required depends only on the desired accuracy — NOT on the number
of dimensions. Whether the problem lives in 10 or 100 dimensions,
the same sample count produces the same quality of estimate.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE CLASSIC EXAMPLE — ESTIMATING π
------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a square of side length 2 centered at the origin,
with a circle of radius 1 inscribed within it.

  Area of circle   = π × 1² = π
  Area of square   = 2 × 2  = 4

  → Ratio of areas = π / 4

If points are thrown uniformly at random inside the square,
the fraction that land inside the circle will converge to π/4.
Therefore:

  π ≈ 4 × (points inside circle / total points)

The more points thrown, the closer the estimate approaches the
true value of π. This relationship is implemented step by step
throughout this project.

------------------------------------------------------------
KEY FORMULA
------------------------------------------------------------

  Error in the estimate ≈ 1 / sqrt(N)

  N = 100         → error ≈ 0.1    (10%)
  N = 10,000      → error ≈ 0.01   (1%)
  N = 1,000,000   → error ≈ 0.001  (0.1%)

To halve the error, the sample size must be quadrupled.
Convergence is slow — but it is guaranteed, and it holds
regardless of the dimensionality of the problem.
