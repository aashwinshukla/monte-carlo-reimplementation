============================================================
  LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS
============================================================

FORMAL DEFINITION:
  In repeated independent tests with the same actual probability p
  of a particular outcome in each test, the chance that the fraction
  of times that outcome occurs differs from p converges to zero
  as the number of trials goes to infinity.

  Stated plainly: the more times an experiment is repeated,
  the closer the observed frequency gets to the true
  underlying probability.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT MEANS IN PRACTICE
------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a fair coin (p = 0.5 for heads).

  After 10 flips        → 70% heads is entirely plausible
  After 100 flips       → results tend toward 55-45
  After 10,000 flips    → very close to 50-50
  After 1,000,000 flips → essentially indistinguishable from 50-50

The law makes no claim about short runs being balanced.
It asserts that long runs WILL converge.

------------------------------------------------------------
TWO VERSIONS OF THE LAW
------------------------------------------------------------

Weak Law of Large Numbers:
  For any small ε > 0, as n → ∞, the probability that the
  sample mean differs from the true mean by more than ε
  approaches 0.
  This is convergence in probability.

Strong Law of Large Numbers:
  The sample mean will almost surely converge to the true mean
  as n → ∞.
  This is almost sure convergence — a strictly stronger statement.

For practical simulation purposes, the weak law captures
everything that matters. The strong law is a theoretical
refinement relevant to formal proofs.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY THIS IS THE FOUNDATION OF MONTE CARLO
------------------------------------------------------------

Monte Carlo simulation is valid precisely because of the
Law of Large Numbers.

In the π estimation experiment:
  The true probability of a random point landing inside
  the quarter-circle is π/4.

  By the Law of Large Numbers:
    Observed fraction → converges to π/4 as N → ∞
    Therefore: 4 × (observed fraction) → converges to π

The law provides the theoretical guarantee that running
more simulations will produce a more accurate result.
Without it, Monte Carlo would have no mathematical foundation.

------------------------------------------------------------
RATE OF CONVERGENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

The Law of Large Numbers guarantees convergence but says
nothing about how fast it occurs. That is given separately
by the standard error formula:

  Error ≈ 1 / sqrt(N)

  N = 100       → error ≈ 10%
  N = 10,000    → error ≈ 1%
  N = 1,000,000 → error ≈ 0.1%

Halving the error requires quadrupling the sample size.
Convergence is slow — but crucially, it is independent
of the dimensionality of the problem.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THE LAW DOES NOT SAY — THE GAMBLER'S FALLACY
------------------------------------------------------------

A common misreading of this law produces what is known
as the Gambler's Fallacy: the belief that after a streak of
one outcome, the opposite outcome is now more likely to
"balance things out."

  "Ten heads in a row — tails is surely due now."

This is incorrect. Each flip is independent. The coin has
no memory of previous outcomes. The probability of tails
on the next flip is still exactly 0.5.

What the law actually describes is dilution, not correction.
The 10-head streak does not get paid back — it gets
mathematically overwhelmed by the volume of future flips,
and the fraction gradually converges toward 0.5.

  After 10 flips      → 10 heads,     0 tails  → 100%    heads
  After 1,000 flips   → ~510 heads, 490 tails  →  51%    heads
  After 100,000 flips → ~50,003 heads           →  50.003% heads

Notably, the absolute difference (heads minus tails) may
actually grow over time — yet the fraction still converges.
That distinction is the entire point.
