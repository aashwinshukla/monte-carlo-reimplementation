============================================================
  REGRESSION TO THE MEAN
============================================================

ORIGIN:
  The term was coined by Francis Galton in 1885.
  Galton was not just a biologist — he was a statistician,
  explorer, and one of the most data-driven thinkers of his era.

------------------------------------------------------------
GALTON'S OBSERVATION
------------------------------------------------------------

Galton collected height data from parents and their adult children.
His finding:

  Two taller-than-average parents → child tends to be shorter than them
  Two shorter-than-average parents → child tends to be taller than them

This wasn't a guess. He had the data to back it up.
He called it "regression toward mediocrity" — what we now call
regression to the mean.

The reason: extreme outcomes (very tall, very short) involve
a combination of genetics AND luck (nutrition, environment, etc.).
The next generation inherits the genes, but gets its own independent
roll of the luck factors. On average, the luck evens out —
pulling the result back toward the population average.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE FORMAL IDEA
------------------------------------------------------------

Following an extreme random event, the next random event
is likely to be less extreme.

This is not the universe "correcting" anything.
It is simply the mathematics of probability:
extreme values are rare by definition, so the next
observation is statistically more likely to be closer to average.

------------------------------------------------------------
ROULETTE EXAMPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

You spin a fair roulette wheel 10 times and get 10 reds in a row.
That is an extreme event. P(10 reds) = (1/2)^10 = 1/1024.

Now consider the next 10 spins.

  Gambler's Fallacy interpretation:
    "I'll get more blacks than usual to even things out."
    → WRONG. Each spin is independent. The wheel has no memory.

  Regression to the Mean interpretation:
    "It is likely I'll get fewer than 10 reds in the next 10 spins."
    → CORRECT. Because 10/10 reds is extreme and unlikely to repeat.
    The expected number is 5 reds.

Now look at the combined 20 spins:
  First 10  → 10 reds  (100%)
  Next 10   → ~5 reds  (50%, expected)
  Total     → ~15/20 = 75% reds

The overall average is moving back toward 50%.
NOT because the wheel compensated — but because extreme events
are rare and unlikely to keep happening.

------------------------------------------------------------
REGRESSION TO THE MEAN vs GAMBLER'S FALLACY
------------------------------------------------------------

These two ideas are easy to confuse. Here's the distinction:

  Gambler's Fallacy:
    Claims the NEXT INDIVIDUAL EVENT is affected by the past.
    "After 10 reds, the next spin is more likely to be black."
    → False. Next spin is still 50/50.

  Regression to the Mean:
    Claims the AVERAGE OVER FUTURE EVENTS will move toward the mean.
    "After 10 reds, the next 10 spins will probably average ~50% red."
    → True. Because extreme streaks don't usually continue.

One is about a single event. The other is about the distribution
of future outcomes. They sound similar but are completely different.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE REGRESSION TO THE MEAN SHOWS UP
------------------------------------------------------------

Sports:
  A player has an extraordinary season (lucky + skilled).
  The next season they "regress" — people say they got worse,
  but often they just returned to their true ability level.
  Coaches get fired after a bad season following a great one —
  the team would have improved naturally either way.

Medicine:
  Patients seek treatment when symptoms are at their WORST.
  They often improve afterward — not necessarily because of
  the treatment, but because extreme symptoms naturally subside.
  This is why placebo-controlled trials are essential.

Testing and Scores:
  A student scores unusually high on one exam (lucky day).
  Their next score will likely be lower — not because they
  got worse, but because the first score was an outlier.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE KEY TAKEAWAY
------------------------------------------------------------

  Extreme events tend to be followed by less extreme events.
  Not because the system is self-correcting —
  but because extreme events are, by definition, unlikely to repeat.

  The mean is not a target the system aims for.
  It is simply the most probable outcome at any given moment.
