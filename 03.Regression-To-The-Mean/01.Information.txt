============================================================
  REGRESSION TO THE MEAN
============================================================

ORIGIN:
  The term was coined by Francis Galton in 1885.
  Galton was a statistician, explorer, and one of the most
  empirically rigorous thinkers of the 19th century.
  His work on heredity and measurement laid foundational
  groundwork for modern statistics.

------------------------------------------------------------
GALTON'S OBSERVATION
------------------------------------------------------------

Galton collected height measurements from parents and their
adult children across a large dataset. The pattern he observed
was consistent and striking:

  Two taller-than-average parents → child tends to be
    shorter than the parents (though still above average)

  Two shorter-than-average parents → child tends to be
    taller than the parents (though still below average)

This was not conjecture — it was a conclusion drawn directly
from data. Galton called it "regression toward mediocrity,"
a term that has since been softened to regression to the mean.

The explanation lies in the structure of extreme outcomes.
A very tall person is tall partly due to genetics, and partly
due to favorable environmental and developmental factors —
nutrition, health during growth, and so on. These secondary
factors are essentially random. The next generation inherits
the genetics but draws independently from the same pool of
random environmental factors. On average, those factors cancel
out, pulling the result back toward the population mean.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE FORMAL PRINCIPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

Following an extreme random event, the next random event
drawn from the same distribution is likely to be less extreme.

This is not the system correcting itself.
It is a direct consequence of probability: extreme values are
rare by definition, and the next observation is simply more
likely to fall near the center of the distribution than at
its tail.

------------------------------------------------------------
ROULETTE AS AN ILLUSTRATION
------------------------------------------------------------

A fair roulette wheel is spun 10 times, producing 10 consecutive
reds. This is an extreme outcome: P(10 reds) = (1/2)^10 = 1/1024.

Two distinct interpretations arise for the next 10 spins:

  Gambler's Fallacy interpretation:
    "The next 10 spins will yield more blacks than usual
     to compensate."
    This is incorrect. Each spin is independent.
    The wheel carries no memory of the previous 10 outcomes.

  Regression to the Mean interpretation:
    "The next 10 spins are unlikely to also produce 10 reds,
     since that is a rare outcome."
    This is correct. The expected outcome for 10 fair spins
    is 5 reds. The probability of again seeing exactly 10 is
    only 1/1024.

Examining the combined 20-spin window:
  First 10  → 10 reds  (100%)
  Next 10   → ~5 reds  (50%, expected value)
  Combined  → ~15/20 = 75% red

The overall proportion moves back toward 50% — not because
the wheel compensated, but because the extreme outcome was
a statistical outlier that was unlikely to repeat.

------------------------------------------------------------
REGRESSION TO THE MEAN vs GAMBLER'S FALLACY
------------------------------------------------------------

These two concepts are frequently conflated. The distinction
is precise and important:

  Gambler's Fallacy:
    Claims the next individual event is influenced by the past.
    "After 10 reds, the next spin is more likely to be black."
    → Incorrect. The next spin is 50/50 regardless.

  Regression to the Mean:
    Claims that the average over future events will return
    toward the distribution mean.
    "After 10 reds, the next 10 spins will probably average
     closer to 50% red."
    → Correct. Extreme streaks are statistically unlikely
      to continue at the same rate.

The former makes a claim about a single event.
The latter makes a claim about a distribution of outcomes.
They are not the same statement, and confusing them leads
to systematically wrong reasoning.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE REGRESSION TO THE MEAN APPEARS
------------------------------------------------------------

Sports Performance:
  A player posts an extraordinary season, outperforming
  historical levels. The following season they "decline."
  This is frequently attributed to reduced effort or skill,
  when in many cases it is simply regression to the mean —
  an unusually strong performance was partly luck, and luck
  does not sustain itself indefinitely.

Medical Trials:
  Patients characteristically seek treatment at the point of
  peak symptom severity. Many improve afterward — regardless
  of whether the treatment is effective. Without a control
  group, it is impossible to separate genuine treatment
  effect from regression to the mean. This is precisely why
  placebo-controlled randomized trials are the standard.

Academic Testing:
  A student scores exceptionally well on one examination —
  a performance influenced partly by genuine ability and
  partly by favorable conditions on that day. The subsequent
  score is likely to be lower, not because ability declined,
  but because the first score was an outlier.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE KEY PRINCIPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

  Extreme events tend to be followed by less extreme events.
  Not because the system is self-correcting —
  but because, by the nature of probability distributions,
  extreme outcomes are rare and unlikely to repeat.

  The mean is not a target the system is drawn toward.
  It is simply the most probable single outcome in any
  given trial drawn from a symmetric distribution.
