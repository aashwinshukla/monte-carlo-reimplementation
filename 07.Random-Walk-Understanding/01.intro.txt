============================================================
  RANDOM WALK
============================================================

A random walk is a mathematical model based on Brownian motion —
the erratic, unpredictable movement of a particle suspended in
a fluid, first formally described by Robert Brown in 1827 and
given a mathematical foundation by Einstein in 1905.

The core idea: at each step, the particle moves in a direction
determined purely by chance. No memory of previous steps.
No preference for any direction. Pure randomness.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHY RANDOM WALKS MATTER
------------------------------------------------------------

Random walks appear across a surprising range of domains:

  Finance:
    Stock prices are often modelled as random walks. Each day's
    price movement is treated as a random step from the previous
    price. This is the foundation of the Efficient Market Hypothesis
    — if all information is already priced in, future movements
    are essentially random.

  Physics:
    Diffusion of particles in a gas or liquid. How far does a
    molecule travel from its origin over time? The answer follows
    directly from random walk mathematics.

  Biology:
    Movement of bacteria searching for food (chemotaxis).
    Foraging patterns of animals in the wild.

  Computer Science:
    Randomised algorithms, network routing, PageRank (Google's
    original algorithm) is built on random walks across a graph.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE CENTRAL QUESTION
------------------------------------------------------------

Given a particle taking N random steps, how far from the origin
does it end up?

Intuition might say: since every step is equally likely in any
direction, the particle should stay near the origin. Steps in
one direction get cancelled out by steps in the opposite direction.

This intuition is partially right but misses something important.
The expected distance from the origin after N steps is not 0 —
it grows with N. Specifically:

  Expected distance ≈ √N

After 100 steps  → expected distance ≈ 10
After 10,000 steps → expected distance ≈ 100

The particle drifts. Not in any particular direction, but away
from the origin. This is a direct consequence of the same
mathematics behind the standard error formula from Chapter 05.

------------------------------------------------------------
THE SIMULATION
------------------------------------------------------------

The simulation constrains movement to four cardinal directions:

  NORTH → (x, y+1)
  SOUTH → (x, y-1)
  EAST  → (x+1, y)
  WEST  → (x-1, y)

At each step, one of these four directions is chosen with equal
probability (1/4 each). The particle's position is updated and
the path is recorded.

Running this thousands of times and averaging the final distances
gives an experimental estimate of the expected distance — which
should converge to √N as the number of trials increases.

This is Monte Carlo applied to geometry: instead of computing
E[distance] analytically, simulate the walk many times and
let the Law of Large Numbers produce the answer.

------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT TO LOOK FOR IN THE RESULTS
------------------------------------------------------------

  Single walk visualisation:
    The path will look chaotic. No discernible pattern.
    But the particle will almost certainly end up away from
    the origin — rarely does it return to (0,0).

  Average distance across many walks:
    This should converge to approximately √N.
    The more walks simulated, the tighter the estimate.

  Maximum distance:
    The furthest the particle travelled during the walk.
    Gives a sense of the range of possible outcomes.

  This is the pattern hidden inside randomness —
  not a direction, but a rate of drift.
